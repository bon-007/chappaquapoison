About This Archive

This is not a memoir, a court filing, a work of journalism, or a blog. It is a documentary narrative compiled from multiple independent archives — created by different people, for different reasons, before this book existed. The observations came first. The record emerged over time. This is one compilation of that record.

A man walks into a relationship with a woman from one of the wealthiest families in Chappaqua, New York. Within a year, he is sick with an illness no doctor can name. Within two, his nanny tells him his partner has been putting drugs in his wine. Within three, his daughter is taken across the country. Within four, a court orders him to erase his own record of what happened.

A jury in San Francisco heard the evidence. Eleven of twelve found the mother liable for battery, domestic violence, and intentional infliction of emotional distress — with a finding of malice. The appellate court affirmed. The judgment was domesticated in New York.

The child is still in Chappaqua.

If the evidence is undeniable — if the fabrications are confessed, the poisoning is documented, and a jury validated the truth — why does the custody order still stand? And why was this record ordered erased?

This page is the answer to that question. It explains what this archive is, where it came from, and — the part most documentary projects leave out — how it accumulated.

What Came First

The most important thing to understand about this archive is the one thing that is easiest to miss: almost none of it was made for this book.

Stevie ♥ Evie, the original Chappaqua Poison blog, A Brie Grows in Brooklyn, the court record, the depositions, the laboratory reports — these were created years earlier, by different people, for different reasons, under different incentives. Some were written in love. One was written by a member of the family, against the family's interest. Some were compelled under oath. One was built, automatically, by an Apple device that had no stake in the outcome.

That independence is the foundation everything else rests on. A skeptical reader is right to ask the obvious question: did the author assemble all of this after deciding to write a book? The answer is no. The record existed first. This narrative is one compilation of it — the latest and most readable, not the only one possible.

So this is not a collection of archives assembled to support a conclusion. It is a documentary ecosystem that already existed — and the narrative is simply its latest, most readable representation.

The Source Archives

Independent. Contemporaneous. Created before this book. Each has its own author, its own purpose, its own origin and its own attempt at silencing — and each remains independently available for inspection.

Independent voices

Stevie ♥ Evie — the family archive

Aunt K compiled hundreds of pages of evidence into physical books, then a blog. Her faith was simple: if people could just see the evidence, they would do the right thing. A Westchester court ordered the blog erased, deactivated, and deleted. A jury listened instead. 203 posts. Corroborated by the court record, the device backup, and the family's own depositions.

Chappaqua Poison (original) — investigative documentation

The earlier public effort to organize the record into a navigable case. 138 posts.

A Brie Grows in Brooklyn — a memoir written against interest

Brienne Walsh — Tara's sister — chronicled the Walsh household in her own words, openly, for years, long before any of this became litigation. She documented what the family would later deny under oath. She is not a friendly witness to her own family; that is precisely what makes her account so hard to dismiss. Years of posts. Corroborated by her own sworn deposition and the family's text messages.

The Podcast — independent journalism

Reporter Michaelanne Petrella investigated the case and began a twelve-episode podcast. Tara Walsh emailed her directly, invoking the gag order, naming Petrella's sister, and attaching a restraining order — using a judicial instrument to silence a journalist. Petrella couldn't continue. The evidence she gathered survived.

The institutional and forensic record

The Court Record — sworn and compelled

Affidavits, discovery, and six depositions, including five members of the Walsh family — testimony given under penalty of perjury and evidence produced under legal compulsion. Three contradictory characterizations of the same court order. The cases are the ultimate public record.

The Laboratory Evidence — forensic

Toxicology documenting lithium, quetiapine (Seroquel), mycophenolic acid, and niacin at concentrations inconsistent with therapeutic use — substances no doctor prescribed.

The Backup — self-authenticating

In 2018, Tara Walsh used Russell's Mac to charge and back up her iPhone. iTunes created a forensic-quality archive — index files for every message, photo, and search, in a structure nearly impossible to fabricate. Years later, Walsh's own attorney produced many of the same messages as bates-stamped discovery, independently authenticating the archive she had sought to suppress. It survived because it was built by Apple, not by anyone with an interest in the outcome.

Why independence becomes proof

A partisan archive, standing alone, can be argued with. Stevie ♥ Evie was written to defend a father; that is no secret. The power of this record is not that any single archive is neutral — it is that the archives are independent of one another, and they converge. No single archive proves the story. But when a family member's memoir, the family's own sworn depositions, the family's own text messages, a forensic device backup, and an independent journalist's reporting all describe the same events — and several of those sources had every reason not to — the convergence of independent and adverse records makes particular claims increasingly difficult to explain away. The same fact, told by archives that had no reason to agree, is how historians and courts establish what happened. Throughout this archive, claims are scored with an Evidence Confidence Score reflecting how many independent sources support them and how strong the documentary basis is.

From Artifacts to Understanding

The source archives are the documentary substrate. Every representation above them exists to make that record easier to explore — without replacing it.

It helps to see the whole chain. Observations came first: what people saw and lived through. Those became artifacts — texts, photographs, recordings, device backups, sworn testimony — the first things that can actually be inspected. Artifacts were gathered into the source archives. And the archives are then represented at progressively higher altitudes: the evidence graph normalizes them, tracing every factual claim to a source artifact, cross-referenced and confidence-scored; the documentary narrative — this site, 54 chapters across 9 acts — is the readable synthesis, each chapter linking back to the source posts and exhibits it was built from; and the Trade Edition is the literary reconstruction for the general reader, one compilation of the documentary record, not a replacement for it.

Each step upward organizes and interprets. Artifacts are the first layer a reader can independently inspect; every representation above them preserves a path back to them. The work here is closer to that of a curator and compiler than an author: the artifacts were made by others; this project recovers, organizes, cross-links, and scores them into a navigable record — and refuses to let them be erased.

Most books ask readers to trust an author. This project attempts the opposite: to reduce the amount of trust required. It asks readers to inspect a record, not to believe a narrator. Every layer above the source archives exists to make the underlying material easier to navigate, not harder to question. Readers are encouraged to move freely between the narrative and its sources, and to reach their own conclusions.

The principle: reversible representation

Every higher-level representation preserves a navigable path back to the documentary artifacts from which it was derived. Representations exist to improve understanding, not to replace evidence. A reader should be able to descend from narrative, to chapter, to source archive, to artifact whenever they wish — and to find the path intact.

Each representation also compresses the record — the Trade Edition most, the evidence graph least, the artifacts hardly at all. The discipline is that the compression stays recoverable: like an archive format that lets you restore the original, not one that discards it. Anything in a representation that cannot be traced back down is not compression. It is invention — and it does not belong here.

Act I

The Fool

A systems architect who built surveillance platforms for the FBI and NCMEC meets a woman from a fiercely private family in Chappaqua, New York. He moves in. He gets sick — cognitive fog, tremors, collapses. No doctor can explain it. Then a lab report arrives: lithium at six times normal.

The Poisoning Revealed

Supervised Visits

The Courts

The New York Family Court becomes the second arena. Steve seeks a protective order. It is granted. How the Record Accumulated — and Survived

Most archives appear fully formed. This one is the opposite: you can watch it being built, in public, over years, after a court ordered it destroyed.

2018 — the events. Drugging, departure, emergency custody, the cross-country move.

2020 — the blogs document it in real time.

2021 — the archives expand. A Westchester court issues the gag order: erase, deactivate, and delete. The record does not disappear. It is organized.

2024–2026 — the evidence is normalized into a sourced, cross-referenced graph.

2026 — the documentary edition.

Next — the Trade Edition.

The gag order sits in the middle of that timeline. The record kept growing after the court ordered it erased. That is not just provenance — it is the spine of the entire project.

Provenance and Transparency

This archive contains only materials derived from public records, filed court documents, previously published content, and documents disclosed during litigation. Every factual claim traces to a source.

Where reconstruction bridges gaps between documents, those passages are marked and scored. Original captures are distinguished from reconstructions. Some posts — promised under "coming soon" headings on the original blogs — were never published before the gag order took the sites offline; where the archive reconstructs them from the underlying evidence, it says so plainly. Honesty about what is original, what is reconstructed, and what was never finished is part of why the record can be trusted.

For Evie

This archive is for Evie.

It exists so that if a father and daughter never meet, she will know who he was. Not what he was accused of. Who he was. An inventor who built things. A man who tried to protect her — and her mother — at extraordinary cost. There is no malice here. No brokenness. Just an exercise in truth-telling by a father who believed the documented record should survive, even when a court ordered it erased.

Every institution involved presented the same proposition: accept the loss quietly, or lose more. Comply with the erasure, or be erased yourself.

This archive is the answer to that no-win scenario: change the conditions of the test. Tell the truth. Source every claim. Cite every document. Let the record speak. Leave it for her.
