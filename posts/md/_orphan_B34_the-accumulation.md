The Reno lab result arrived and Steve sat with it and did what he had been trained to do in another life: he looked at the data.

Not a single data point. The whole set.

---

**Discovery One.** The illness.

Two thousand sixteen through two thousand seventeen. The years when Steve could not think clearly, when his body produced symptoms that three physicians attributed to Lyme disease, chronic fatigue, babesia — diagnoses that explained the fatigue and the confusion but not the pattern. Not the way the symptoms arrived when he was home and departed when he traveled. Not the way his clarity returned in hotels and airports and other people's offices, and vanished again each evening in the apartment on Vermont Street.

**Discovery Two.** The Brooklyn night.

Two thousand seventeen. The evening in the apartment on Atlantic Avenue when Steve took his Adderall — the same Adderall he had taken for years — and the world detached. Not the focused clarity the medication usually produced but something else: a dissociative fog, a sensation of being present inside a body that was no longer connected to the mind directing it.

The tamper screws on the pill bottles had been at eleven o'clock when he left. They were not at eleven o'clock when he returned.

A reaction to medication, possibly exhaustion, possibly paranoia. No test was run that night. No sample was preserved. The Brooklyn episode lived in the space between what Steve experienced and what could be proven.

The confession was not a test result. It was a witness — a person who had been inside the household, who had seen what was happening, who had chosen to speak.
