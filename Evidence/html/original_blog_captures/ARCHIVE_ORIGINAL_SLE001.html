Court Upholds Verdict in Walsh v. Russell, Setting Precedents for Implied Consent in Drugging, Battery, and Domestic Violence
The First District Court recently affirmed the judgment against Walsh in a complex case involving charges of battery, intentional infliction of emotional distress, and domestic violence.





In a decision that legal experts say will have far-reaching implications, the court recently affirmed the judgment against Walsh in a complex case involving charges of battery, intentional infliction of emotional distress, and domestic violence. The case revolved around Walsh's drugging of Russell without his consent.Battery and the Question of 'Implied Consent'One of the most salient aspects of this case is Walsh's defense against the battery charge, where she posited that Russell had rendered "implied consent" to being drugged. The court's analysis was unequivocal in dismissing this argument, stating that, "Walsh argues only that Russell rendered implied consent. Unaided by Piedra and Saxena, Walsh’s argument challenging the sufficiency of the evidence for battery comprises a series of assertions unsupported by any legal authority or citations to the record."Walsh cited two cases in her defense: Piedra v. Dugan (2004) 123 Cal.App.4th 1483 and Saxena v. Goffney (2008) 159 Cal.App.4th 316. However, the court pointed out that these were irrelevant to her argument. According to the judgment, "In both Piedra and Saxena, the defendants were medical doctors and the legal analysis in those cases related to signed consent forms by which the plaintiffs or their guardians expressly consented to medical treatment."In doing so, the court essentially closed the door on the loosely-defined concept of "implied consent" as a valid legal defense in cases involving allegations of battery, especially where medication or drugging is concerned. This narrows the scope for defendants in future similar cases to rely on the ambiguity of implied consent.Intentional Infliction of Emotional DistressThe judgment also delved deep into the sufficiency of the evidence for the intentional infliction of emotional distress. Citing Christensen v. Superior Court (1991) 54 Cal.3d 868, 903, the court listed the elements of this tort as: “(1) extreme and outrageous conduct by the defendant with the intention of causing, or reckless disregard of the probability of causing, emotional distress; (2) the plaintiff’s suffering severe or extreme emotional distress; and (3) actual and proximate causation of the emotional distress by the defendant’s outrageous conduct.”The court then elaborated, "Here, Walsh’s extreme and outrageous conduct consisted of her drugging Russell’s wine without his knowledge or consent." It further established Walsh's culpability by indicating that she had “reckless disregard for the probability of causing emotional distress,” as she was aware of Russell's belief that he was being drugged and the distress it was causing him.The Legal Landscape of Domestic ViolenceDomestic violence was another key issue addressed in the verdict. According to Civil Code section 1708.6, "A person is liable for the tort of domestic violence if the plaintiff proves both of the following elements: [¶] (1) The infliction of injury upon the plaintiff resulting from abuse, as defined in subdivision (a) of Section 13700 of the Penal Code. [¶] (2) The abuse was committed by the defendant, a person having a relationship with the plaintiff."The court concluded that Walsh had committed abuse by placing Russell "in reasonable apprehension of imminent serious bodily injury," referencing Penal Code, § 13700, subd. (a). This led to the symptoms of "withdrawal and neuropathy" that Russell experienced.The court explained that Walsh offered another explanation for those symptoms, namely Russell’s pre-existing "mental health issues," but rebuffed it by stating, "‘where two or more different inferences can reasonably be drawn from the evidence, this court is without power to substitute its own inferences for those of the [trier of fact].’" citing Escobar v. Flores (2010) 183 Cal.App.4th 737, 752.Final Judgment and Its ImplicationsThe court's final disposition was a complete affirmation of the initial judgment: "The judgment is affirmed. Russell is entitled to recover his costs on appeal."By upholding the verdict on all counts, the court has made a profound statement about the sufficiency of evidence needed in similar cases, especially involving battery, emotional distress, and domestic violence. Legal scholars and analysts are pointing to the case's implications on the standards of "implied consent" and how it could affect future legal arguments in similar situations.This case serves as a critical reference point for those advocating for stricter interpretations of consent and provides a roadmap for legal practitioners navigating the intricate issues of emotional distress and domestic violence.Given the complexity and the sensitivity of the issues at hand, this decision is not only legally significant but also serves as a social commentary on the standards of conduct expected in relationships and broader society.
